Helping hotels and venues turn inquiries into booked business

Line

Making group hospitality bookable

Role: Co-founder & CEO · Product strategy · Product design

My co-founders and I quit our jobs to build Line. And truthfully, there has to be something wrong with people who do this. We traded healthy, seemingly safe salaries and stability for a journey that includes failure, rejection, and isolation in order to bring something new to market.

So, naturally, the question is why do it? Simply: because it felt like a problem worth solving.

I spent the first part of my career in hospitality before moving into software, product, and design. Years later, when I started looking closely at group hospitality again, I was surprised by how familiar everything felt. Not familiar in a nostalgic way. Familiar in a wait, we're still doing this? way.

A problem thinly disguised as convention

Some problems hang around for so long that people stop treating them as problems and start calling them “the process.” Group hospitality is one of them.

A guest planning a company retreat, wedding, meeting, private dinner, or room block is basically trying to answer three questions:

  • What's available?
  • What will it cost?
  • Does it feel right?

Instead, they get websites, forms, PDFs, emails, RFP portals, phone calls, and a lot of waiting.

The property isn't having a much better time. On the other side, someone is qualifying the inquiry, checking calendars, figuring out pricing, coordinating internally, building a proposal, following up, and eventually putting together a contract.

The guest wants to buy. The property wants to sell. The friction is everything sitting between them.

The case for Line

At first glance, the obvious problem was the form. Get rid of the form, make the experience conversational, respond faster. All true.

But the deeper we went, the more obvious it became that the form was just the visible part of a larger problem: supply itself wasn't legible.

A property's pricing logic, availability, rules, exceptions, spaces, terms, and contracts lived across documents, inboxes, legacy systems, spreadsheets, and human judgment rather than in a form software could reliably understand and act on.

Demand could ask. Supply couldn't really answer.

That became the thesis behind Line:

  • Supply creates the market.
  • Pricing follows workflow ownership.
  • Distribution follows structured supply.

If supply becomes structured, executable, and programmable, things that weren't previously possible become possible: real-time pricing, real availability, qualification, proposals, transactions, and eventually a market that actually clears.

The vision was large. We needed a much smaller place to start.

v0: Prove value in production

We started at the first click.

The traditional group sales experience begins with a static inquiry form. We replaced it with a conversation. Line learned the property's spaces, pricing, policies, imagery, and constraints, then used that information to answer questions, understand what the guest wanted, qualify the opportunity, and connect them with the operator.

v0 was deliberately constrained. We weren't trying to automate group sales end to end. We were trying to test a handful of foundational assumptions:

  • Could we structure property knowledge well enough for software to act on it?
  • Would guests engage with a conversation instead of a form?
  • Could the system reliably understand pricing, availability, and fit?
  • Would operators trust it with real inquiries?
  • Would any of this actually make the workflow better?

We shipped v0 in the fall and ended the year with our first live deployment. One of the first inquiries went from guest message to qualified lead and scheduled site visit in roughly five seconds, without operator involvement or an RFP.

The operator messaged me: “a real lead that booked a tour all through the ai. So that is cool that it's already been successful in doing that for me!!”

That deployment became our first paying customer.

Read the first customer story →

Then the behavior started repeating. By the end of v0:

  • 5 deployment partners
  • 4 paying customers
  • 700+ deals
  • 45%+ handled after hours
  • 80%+ inquiry capture
  • autonomous qualification and site-visit scheduling

At one point across seven live properties, 91% of guests responded, 64% of inquiries became qualified opportunities, and operators were getting more than ten hours a week back from repetitive coordination.

The after-hours number was always particularly interesting to me. The software wasn't simply doing the same job faster. It was doing something a person literally couldn't do while they were asleep.

v0 proved the wedge. It also showed us exactly where the product broke next.

Starting the deal was easy. Closing it was the problem.

A lot of Line was designed by sitting inside hotel sales offices and watching people work.

Line could capture and qualify the opportunity, but once the deal became real, everything fragmented again across email, calendars, PDFs, proposal tools, contracts, legacy systems, and manual follow-up.

I kept hearing versions of the same thing: “I'm chained to my desk.” “This isn't hospitality anymore.” “I don't want to be a data clerk.”

And the product requests started clustering around the same gaps:

  • I need availability in here.
  • I need to send the proposal from here.
  • I don't want to go back to email.
  • Can it generate the contract?
  • What is actually closing?

At some point those stop being feature requests. They're telling you where the boundary of the product is wrong.

That defined v1.

How we built

The way we worked was tightly connected to that learning loop.

Rather than design a future version of Line separately and hand it off, I prototyped workflows directly in the repo using the same design system and components as the production application. That let me work through interactions with real constraints and get much closer to the final experience than I could with a static prototype.

Once a direction felt right, I would work with the team to break it into smaller iterations:

  • What needs to be real now?
  • What can we mock or defer?
  • What do we need to learn?
  • What's the smallest version we can put in front of an operator?

Then we built it, deployed it, watched what happened, and used that evidence to determine the next pass.

Prototype → align → break down → build → deploy → learn → repeat

The design system lived alongside the product, so patterns we proved once could become reusable building blocks for whatever came next.

That mattered even more with AI. A mockup can tell you whether a button makes sense. It can't tell you whether an agent will invent a wedding fee that doesn't exist, whether an operator trusts a qualification, or whether a guest gives up halfway through a conversation.

You have to put it in the world. Then the world tells you what you got wrong.

v1: Own the workflow that closes bookings

By then the product question had changed from Can Line answer an inquiry? to Can a coordinator take an inquiry all the way to signed contract without leaving Line?

If not, Line was another tool. If yes, Line could become the system.

The same structured supply that powered v0 became the foundation for the rest of the workflow. v1 expanded into:

  • RFP intake and qualification
  • availability and room blocks
  • pricing and program building
  • proposals
  • in-app messaging
  • contracts and e-signatures
  • reporting and the learning loop

The old workflow looked something like:

forms → waiting → manual qualification → PDFs → follow-up → contract

We wanted it to become:

intent → fit → pricing → availability → proposal → booking

The important change wasn't adding more features. It was workflow ownership.

Every step Line owned meant less context switching, less data entry, and less coordination for the operator. Over time, the operator could move from doing the work toward directing and approving it.

Our design principle became Dieter Rams' weniger, aber besser: less, but better.

The goal wasn't to give operators another long list of features. It was to remove the things they had to do:

  • qualify the lead
  • check availability
  • build the program
  • create the room block
  • build the proposal
  • draft the contract
  • run the report

Our feature was removing their features.

The product arc eventually became clear:

Structured supply → instant response → deal flow → commercial logic → programmable supply.

Configure the property once. Let it answer demand wherever demand appears.

Where it was going

We started with the property because supply had to become legible first.

Demand has had listing sites, marketplaces, planner tools, and RFP networks for decades. Yet the market still doesn't really clear because none of those products can expose information they don't own: what's available, what's flexible, what should this cost, what requires approval, what terms matter, and which business is actually worth taking.

The property knows. The problem is that today the answer still lives across humans and documents.

Structure that commercial truth and suddenly the same supply can serve far more than one interface: websites, marketplaces, planning tools, AI agents, and channels that haven't been invented yet.

That was the long-term bet behind Line: make supply programmable so demand can finally meet it on demand.

Where we landed

We made real product progress. Line went from a fairly simple idea about replacing a form to hundreds of real deals, paying customers, properties in multiple countries, hospitality integrations, and a product increasingly capable of moving an opportunity from first message toward contract.

We proved a lot of things I wasn't sure we could prove when we started.

But product progress and company economics aren't the same thing.

Hospitality is a trust-driven industry. Sales cycles are long, integrations take time, early deployments are hands-on, and customer acquisition, at least in the short term, was expensive. As a bootstrapped company, the path was simply going to take longer than we could responsibly support with families and lives outside of the startup.

The constraint had shifted from Can we build this? to Can we distribute it economically and fast enough?

I also came away believing even more strongly that this market eventually curves toward the demand side. But supply has to become structured first.

Which leads to the least original startup lesson of all time. Unfortunately, it also seems to be true:

Distribution is everything.

Line changed how I think about building products. The vision can be enormous. The first product probably shouldn't be.

Find the smallest place where you can create real value. Put it into the real workflow. Watch what happens. And when it breaks, pay attention.

That's usually the product telling you where to go next.
