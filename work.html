Helping hotels and venues turn inquiries into booked business

Opening the group bookings market

Role: Co-founder & CEO

My co-founders and I all quit our jobs to build Line. And truthfully, there has to be something wrong with people who do this. We traded healthy, seemingly safe salaries and stability for a journey that includes failure, rejection, and isolation in order to bring something new to market.

So, naturally, the question is why do it? Simply: because it felt like a problem worth solving.

When I was learning web development and design, I worked in hospitality and experienced this problem firsthand. More than a decade later, I went back into the field to understand how those workflows had changed and found them messier than ever. Weddings, corporate meetings, retreats, private dining, room blocks, and events still moved through forms, email, PDFs, phone calls, and manual coordination.

We started building Line to make a better way for operators to book business.

The problem

Group bookings have two sides: the guest trying to find the right place and the property trying to decide whether the business fits.

The guest is usually trying to answer three things:

  • Pricing
  • Availability
  • Vibes

The property is working through its own version of the same questions:

  • Is it available?
  • What should we charge?
  • Is this a good piece of business?

The answers live across websites, calendars, rate sheets, sales systems, contracts, PDFs, inboxes, spreadsheets, and people.

The guest wants to buy. The property wants to sell. A lot of manual work sits between them.

The market and thesis

Group hospitality is a $1.3 trillion category, but much of the information required to transact is still difficult for software to access. Availability, pricing logic, rules, constraints, spaces, terms, and contracts are distributed across systems, documents, and human knowledge.

Our thesis: structure the supply and more of the market can happen on demand.

The broader thinking behind Line is captured in three pieces:

  • Supply creates the market.
  • Pricing follows workflow ownership.
  • Distribution follows structured supply.

The opportunity was large. We needed a small place to start.

v0: Prove value in production

We started with the first interaction between the guest and the property.

The traditional experience begins with a static inquiry form. Line replaced it with a conversation built around structured information about the property: spaces, pricing, policies, imagery, rules, and constraints.

A guest could ask questions, explain what they were planning, and get an immediate response. Line collected the booking details, qualified the opportunity, and handed structured demand to the operator.

v0 focused on a few questions:

  • Could we structure property knowledge well enough to use it reliably?
  • Would guests engage with the experience?
  • Could Line qualify real inquiries?
  • Would operators trust the result?
  • Would it save meaningful time?

We shipped v0 in the fall and ended the year with our first live deployment.

One of the first inquiries moved from guest message to qualification and a scheduled site visit in roughly five seconds, with no operator involvement.

That deployment became our first paying customer.

Customers and results

The pattern started repeating as we deployed Line with more properties.

By the end of v0:

  • 5 deployment partners
  • 4 paying customers
  • 700+ deals
  • 80%+ inquiry capture
  • 45%+ handled after hours
  • autonomous qualification and site-visit scheduling

Across seven live properties, we also saw a 91% guest response rate, 64% of inquiries become qualified opportunities, and more than 10 hours a week of repetitive work returned to operators.

The after-hours usage became an especially useful signal. Nearly half of the inquiries could arrive when the sales team was not sitting at a desk, and Line could still capture and move the opportunity forward.

The deployments showed us where the workflow went next.

Once an opportunity was qualified, the operator moved back into email, calendars, availability systems, proposals, documents, contracts, and manual follow-up.

The same needs kept surfacing:

  • availability inside Line
  • proposal generation
  • messaging
  • contracts
  • deal-flow visibility

Starting the deal was working. The next job was helping close it.

How we worked

I prototyped Line directly in the repo using the same design system and components as the production application. That gave me a fast way to explore a workflow in context, share it with the team, and break it into smaller iterations.

For each pass we asked:

  • What needs to work now?
  • What can wait?
  • What do we need to learn?
  • What is the smallest useful version we can deploy?

Then we built it, put it in front of operators, watched what happened, and used that to shape the next iteration.

Prototype → align → break down → build → deploy → learn → repeat

The design system lived with the product, so patterns we developed could be reused as the application grew.

v1: Own the workflow that closes bookings

v1 extended Line from the initial inquiry through the deal.

The structured information that powered v0 became the foundation for the full booking workflow.

The product expanded into:

  • RFP intake and qualification
  • availability and room blocks
  • pricing and program building
  • proposals
  • in-app messaging
  • contracts and e-signatures
  • reporting
  • learning across the workflow

The workflow became:

Intent → fit → pricing → availability → proposal → contract

Our design principle was weniger, aber besser: less, but better.

We looked at the work an operator had to perform and kept asking what Line could take off their plate:

  • qualify the inquiry
  • check availability
  • build the program
  • create the room block
  • build the proposal
  • draft the contract
  • run the report

The goal was simple:

Can a coordinator take an inquiry to signed contract without leaving Line?

The product progression became:

Structured supply → instant response → deal flow → commercial logic → programmable supply

Vision

As more of the property became structured, the same supply could support more places where demand originates: a hotel's website, a marketplace, a corporate planning tool, a travel platform, or an agent.

The property could configure its spaces, pricing, availability, rules, and commercial logic once, then make that supply available wherever demand appeared.

That was the broader direction for Line: make group hospitality supply programmable and bookable.
