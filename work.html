Helping hotels and venues turn inquiries into booked business

Opening the group bookings market

Role: Co-founder & CEO

My co-founders and I all quit our jobs to build Line.

The idea started much earlier. When I was learning web development and design, I worked in hospitality and experienced the group sales process firsthand. More than a decade later, I went back into the field to understand how those workflows had changed and found them messier than ever. Weddings, corporate meetings, retreats, private dining, room blocks, and events still moved through forms, email, PDFs, phone calls, and manual coordination.

I brought what I was seeing back to Eli and Katia, who I'd been building with for years at Codecov, later acquired by Sentry. As I mapped the workflows and shared what operators were telling me, we started working through how the process could be rebuilt.

The problem

At Line, we focused on the work between a group inquiry and booked business.

Group bookings are simple in theory. A guest wants to understand pricing, availability, and vibes. The property needs to understand the same basic things to decide what it can sell, what to charge, and whether the business fits.

In practice, those answers are scattered across websites, calendars, rate sheets, sales systems, contracts, PDFs, inboxes, spreadsheets, and people. Every inquiry becomes a coordination problem.

Supply isn't legible to demand, so the market stays closed.

Market, thesis, and vision

Group business in hospitality is a $1.3 trillion category that still runs on manual workflows inside a closed and opaque marketplace. The core supply inputs, availability, pricing logic, constraints, and contracts, live across systems, documents, and human knowledge rather than as machine-readable infrastructure.

When supply becomes structured, executable, and programmable, more of the market can happen on demand. Pricing and availability become easier to expose, workflows become executable, and supply can eventually be distributed wherever demand originates.

Line was designed to be that supply layer: structure the property once, use it to run the booking workflow, and make that supply available across demand channels.

The broader thinking behind Line:

  • Supply creates the market.
  • Pricing follows workflow ownership.
  • Distribution follows structured supply.

The long-term direction was to move from intake and qualification, through collaboration and transaction, toward supply that is machine-readable and available to any distribution channel.

v0: Prove value in production

We started with the first interaction between the guest and the property.

Line replaced the static inquiry form with a conversation built on structured property knowledge: spaces, pricing, policies, imagery, rules, and constraints. Guests could ask questions, share what they were planning, and get an immediate response. Line captured the details, qualified the opportunity, and handed the operator a structured lead.

v0 was designed to answer a few basic questions:

  • Could we structure property knowledge well enough to use it reliably?
  • Would guests engage with the experience?
  • Would operators trust it with real inquiries?

We shipped v0 in the fall and ended the year with our first live deployment.

One of the first inquiries went from the initial guest message to a qualified opportunity and scheduled site visit in roughly five seconds, without operator involvement.

That deployment became our first paying customer.

Customers and results

The pattern started repeating as we deployed Line with more properties.

By the end of v0:

  • 5 deployment partners
  • 4 paying customers
  • 700+ deals
  • 80%+ inquiry capture
  • 45%+ handled after hours
  • autonomous qualification and site-visit scheduling

Across seven live properties, we also saw a 91% guest response rate, 64% of inquiries become qualified opportunities, and more than 10 hours a week of repetitive work returned to operators.

The after-hours usage became an especially useful signal. Nearly half of the inquiries could arrive when the sales team was not sitting at a desk, and Line could still capture and move the opportunity forward.

The deployments showed us where the workflow went next.

Once an opportunity was qualified, the operator moved back into email, calendars, availability systems, proposals, documents, contracts, and manual follow-up.

The same needs kept surfacing:

  • availability inside Line
  • proposal generation
  • messaging
  • contracts
  • deal-flow visibility

Starting the deal was working. The next job was helping close it.

How we worked

I prototyped Line directly in the repo using the same design system and components as the production application. That gave me a fast way to explore a workflow in context, share it with the team, and break it into smaller iterations.

For each pass we asked:

  • What needs to work now?
  • What can wait?
  • What do we need to learn?
  • What is the smallest useful version we can deploy?

Then we built it, put it in front of operators, watched what happened, and used that to shape the next iteration.

Prototype → align → break down → build → deploy → learn → repeat

The design system lived with the product, so patterns we developed could be reused as the application grew.

v1: Own the workflow that closes bookings

v1 extended Line from the initial inquiry through the deal.

The structured information that powered v0 became the foundation for the full booking workflow.

The product expanded into:

  • RFP intake and qualification
  • availability and room blocks
  • pricing and program building
  • proposals
  • in-app messaging
  • contracts and e-signatures
  • reporting
  • learning across the workflow

The workflow became:

Intent → fit → pricing → availability → proposal → contract

Our design principle was weniger, aber besser: less, but better.

We looked at the work an operator had to perform and kept asking what Line could take off their plate:

  • qualify the inquiry
  • check availability
  • build the program
  • create the room block
  • build the proposal
  • draft the contract
  • run the report

The goal was simple:

Can a coordinator take an inquiry to signed contract without leaving Line?

The product progression became:

Structured supply → instant response → deal flow → commercial logic → programmable supply
