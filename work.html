My co-founders and I quit our jobs to build Line. And truthfully, there has to be something wrong with people who do this.

We traded healthy salaries and stability for a journey that includes failure, rejection, and isolation in order to bring something new to market.

So, naturally, the question is why do it?

Simply: because it felt like a problem worth solving.

I spent the first part of my career in hospitality before moving into product and design. Years later, I came back to an industry where booking group business still looked remarkably familiar.

A guest planning a company retreat, wedding, meeting, or event is basically trying to figure out three things: pricing, availability, and vibes.

Instead, they get forms and waiting.

The property isn't having a much better time. On the other side, someone is qualifying inquiries, checking availability, figuring out pricing, coordinating internally, building proposals, following up, and eventually putting together a contract.

We know it's broken. They know it's broken. Everyone knows everyone knows it's broken.

And yet the charade carries on.

That was the problem we wanted to work on.

The problem

What looked like a bad form experience was really a deeper market problem.

Demand could generate an inquiry, but supply was still a black box of pricing, availability, rules, exceptions, and contracts. That commercial knowledge lived across documents, inboxes, legacy systems, spreadsheets, and human judgment rather than in a form software could reliably understand and act on.

That keeps the market closed. Demand can ask, but it can't easily know what is actually available, what it costs, whether it fits, or what it takes to book.

The case for Line

Our thesis was that once supply becomes structured, executable, and programmable, the market can work very differently.

Supply creates the market.

Pricing follows workflow ownership.

Distribution follows structured supply.

The vision was large.

We needed a much smaller place to start.

How we worked

We built Line directly in the product.

Rather than designing everything separately and handing it off, I prototyped new workflows in the repo using the same design system and components as the production application.

That let me explore the actual interaction with real constraints and get much closer to the final experience than I could with a static prototype.

Once a direction felt right, I would work with the team to break it into iterations. We decided what needed to be real now, what we could defer, built it, deployed it with operators, watched what happened, and used that to shape the next pass.

Prototype in product → align → break into iterations → build → deploy → learn → repeat.

The design system lived alongside the product, so patterns we proved once became reusable building blocks for whatever came next.

That mattered even more with AI. A mockup could tell us whether an interface made sense. It couldn't tell us whether an agent would understand a real inquiry, whether an operator would trust the result, or where a real workflow would break.

We needed the product in production to learn.

v0: Prove value in production

We started at the first click.

The traditional group sales experience begins with a static inquiry form. We replaced it with a conversation.

Line learned a property's spaces, pricing, policies, visuals, and constraints, then used that knowledge to answer questions, understand what the guest wanted, qualify the inquiry, and connect them with the operator.

The point of v0 wasn't to automate group sales end to end.

It was to test the foundational assumptions as quickly as possible.

  • Could we structure property knowledge well enough for software to act on it?
  • Would guests engage with it?
  • Could it understand pricing, availability, and fit?
  • Would operators trust it with real inquiries?

We shipped v0 in the fall and ended the year with our first deployment live.

One of the first real inquiries went from guest message to qualification and a scheduled site visit in roughly five seconds, without operator involvement or an RFP.

That deployment became our first paying customer.

Read the first customer story →

As we deployed more properties, the behavior repeated.

By the end of v0, Line had 5 deployment partners, 4 paying customers, and 700+ real deals, with roughly 45% handled after hours.

The system captured inquiries, turned them into structured booking details, qualified opportunities autonomously, and scheduled site visits without requiring someone to sit at a desk waiting for the lead.

v0 proved the wedge.

It also showed us exactly where the product broke next.

What we learned

I spent a lot of time inside group sales offices watching the workflow happen in real time.

The pattern became obvious.

Starting the deal was easy. Closing it was the problem.

Once Line qualified the inquiry, the deal often left the system.

The operator went back to email, spreadsheets, calendars, proposal tools, PDFs, legacy systems, and manual follow-up.

We heard versions of the same requests again and again.

“I need the availability in here.”

“I need to send the proposal from here.”

“I don't want to go back to email.”

“Can it generate the contract?”

“What is actually closing?”

Those weren't feature requests around the edges.

They defined the next product.

v1: Own the workflow that closes bookings

The product question changed.

Instead of asking whether Line could handle the first interaction, we asked:

Can a coordinator take a group inquiry to signed contract without leaving Line?

If not, Line was another tool.

If yes, Line could become the system.

The same structured supply that powered the initial conversation became the foundation for the rest of the workflow.

Line expanded into RFP intake, qualification, availability, room blocks, pricing, proposals, messaging, contracts, e-signatures, reporting, and a learning loop across each interaction.

The workflow moved from:

forms → waiting → manual qualification → PDFs → follow-up → contract

toward:

intent → fit → pricing → availability → proposal → booking

The important change wasn't adding more features.

It was workflow ownership.

Every step Line owned meant less context switching, less data entry, and less manual coordination. The operator could increasingly move from doing the work to directing and approving it.

Our design principle became weniger, aber besser: less, but better.

The goal wasn't to give operators another set of features.

It was to remove the things they had to do.

  • Qualify the lead.
  • Check availability.
  • Build the program.
  • Create the room block.
  • Build the proposal.
  • Draft the contract.
  • Run the report.

Our feature was removing their features.

The product arc had become clear:

Structured supply → instant response → deal flow → commercial logic → programmable supply.

Configure the supply once, then make it available on demand.

Where it was going

We started on the supply side because supply had to become legible first.

Once pricing, availability, rules, constraints, and contracts become structured and executable, that supply can answer demand wherever demand happens to originate.

The end state wasn't a better RFP.

It was a market where a guest could express intent and get fit, pricing, availability, and a path to booking on demand.

Where we landed

We made real product progress.

Line went from an idea about replacing a form to software handling hundreds of real group inquiries, paying customers, international deployments, hospitality integrations, and a much clearer understanding of how this market could work.

But product progress and business economics are different things.

Hospitality is a trust-driven market. Sales cycles are long. Integrations take time. Early deployments require hands-on work. Customer acquisition was expensive in the short term, and as a bootstrapped company, the path was going to take longer than we could responsibly support with families to support.

The constraint eventually shifted from:

Can we build this?

to:

Can we distribute it economically and fast enough?

I also came away believing that the market eventually curves toward the demand side.

Supply has to become structured first. But once it is programmable, the larger opportunity is connecting that supply to demand wherever the customer already happens to be.

Which brings me to the least original lesson from the whole experience, but probably the most important:

Distribution is everything.

Line changed how I think about building products.

The vision can be enormous.

The first product probably shouldn't be.

Find the smallest place where you can create real value. Put it into the actual workflow. Measure what happens. Watch where it breaks. Then let the evidence determine what the product earns the right to own next.
